Sleep on the 24/48: the complete guide

Twenty-four hours on duty, forty-eight off, and the whole thing starts again on day four. This is the reference for the people who live on it: how to sleep the tour when the tones own your wake time, how to survive the drive home, and why the two days off are not the same day and never have been. One

The 24/48 in one paragraph

You go on duty at shift change, you stay on duty for twenty-four hours, you get relieved, and then you have two days before you do it again. Three days, on repeat, forever. On paper it looks like two days off. It is nothing of the sort. The two off days are not the same day. The first starts with you already awake, already carrying whatever the night took. The second starts rested and ends with a 24 in front of you. Same box on the calendar, two entirely different jobs. Almost everything that goes wrong on this schedule goes wrong because somebody treated day one like day two.

That split is the spine of this guide. The day you come off the truck is a recovery day: short aligned sleep, one deliberate nap, an early night. The second day off is a reset day: the full night, on the rest anchor, protecting the night before you go back. Every rule that follows hangs off it.

Two

The tour itself

Sleep on a 24 is not a plan. It is a series of opportunities that may or may not arrive, and the only thing you control is what you do with each one when it shows up. So the tour breaks into four decisions: what you do before it, how you nap during it, when you drink the coffee, and what you do at three in the morning.

The pre-shift nap

The cheapest thing on this whole page. A nap before the tour is not indulgence, it is banking, and it is one of the interventions the American Academy of Sleep Medicine named in its 2025 shift work disorder guideline (AASM clinical practice guideline, 2025). Take it four to five and a half hours after you wake, which on most 24/48 anchors lands late morning, before you leave the house. Twenty to thirty minutes, or a full ninety if you have the room. The window matters: a nap that late lands in the natural dip and costs the least from the night that follows.

Decide before you lie down that this nap is part of the work. The version where you skip it because you feel fine at ten in the morning is the version where you feel fine at ten in the morning and terrible at four the next.

Sleeping between calls, when the tones own your wake

Here is where most sleep advice falls apart. It tells you to protect your sleep window, to ease into your first twenty minutes awake, to avoid abrupt wake-ups. None of that survives contact with a station. On duty you do not choose when you wake, because waking up is the job arriving. The radio is the alarm and it does not negotiate.

So work with the constraint instead of wishing it away. The on-call rule: nap short, so the wake comes from shallow sleep. Twenty to thirty minutes keeps you mostly in light sleep, so when the tones drop you surface from something shallow rather than getting ripped out of the deepest part of a cycle. That grogginess after a bad wake is sleep inertia, and its depth depends on where in the cycle you were when it hit. You cannot control whether the call comes. You can control how far down you are when it does.

If you do bet on a longer block, bet on a full ninety minutes so a cycle can finish, and only when the night has genuinely gone quiet. Anything between thirty and ninety is the worst of both: deep enough to feel wrecked, short enough to have gained little.

And if the tour is broken enough that one long window never happens, split it. Across a simulated sixteen-hour night, a 90-minute nap plus a later 30-minute nap held alertness as well as a single 120-minute block, and on some measures better (Scientific Reports, 2023). The two-hour break is the one nobody actually gets. Two smaller windows you can defend beat one perfect window you cannot.

From the field: Jocko Willink's version is a six-to-eight-minute power nap, feet elevated above the heart. Short enough to stay shallow, and the fixed ritual — same posture, same short duration — is part of why it works. Practice, not a trial.

Two unglamorous things that do more than any of the above. Get horizontal even when you are sure you will not sleep, because lying down in a dark room and not sleeping still beats sitting up in the dayroom under the lights. And leave your boots where you can find them without turning on anything bright, so a two-minute turnout does not also cost you a face full of overhead fluorescents.

Caffeine on a 24

First cup 60 to 90 minutes after waking; last cup 8 hours before the sleep you are protecting

Caffeine works by blocking adenosine, the sleep-pressure chemical. It does not remove the pressure; it hides it, and the debt is waiting when it wears off. Its half-life is about five to six hours on average, faster for some and slower for others depending on a liver-enzyme gene, which is why your partner can have an espresso at dinner and you cannot. (Walker, Huberman Lab Guest Series Ep. 2, 59:25)

On a 24 the arithmetic gets awkward, because the sleep you are protecting is not the one at the end of the tour, it is the one after it. Work backward from that target: eight hours before it, the cup stops. So the last real caffeine of the tour lands in the evening, and the 0200 cup that feels mandatory is the one that costs you the recovery day. Caffeine at three in the morning is still measurably in you at eleven. If you need it anyway, at least make that trade knowingly.

The guideline puts caffeine before or during the shift on its list of things that help. Nobody put it at hour twenty.

0300 to 0500, named honestly

Somewhere in that stretch your circadian rhythm hits its lowest point of the entire twenty-four hours, and it does it at the same moment your sleep pressure is highest, because you have been awake around twenty hours. Both systems point down. This is the hour when the coffee stops working, when you read the same page twice, when the drug math takes longer than it should.

I have run a cardiac arrest at 0400 and I have also sat in a recliner at 0400 unable to remember what I came into the room for. Same body, same night. The trough does not care how good you are at your job.

What you do about it is not heroism. You lean on the systems built for exactly this: the checklist, the protocol, the partner who reads the dose back. That is what they are for. Keep the lights up during the call and drop them after. Do not make an unforced decision at 0400 that could wait for 0700. And when the tour ends, treat everything that happened in that window as having happened to a version of you who was impaired. Because it did.

Three

The morning after: the drive, then the recovery day

The drive home is the most dangerous part of the tour

Before any recovery protocol applies, you have to get home, and this is where people on this schedule actually get hurt. Drowsy driving impairs you the way drinking does. Put a number on it: when researchers ran nurses through a driving simulator on their real commute after a real shift, the night-shift group crashed on 11 percent of drives against 7 percent for the day-shift group, with more drifting out of lane and more of the hard braking that means you saw it late (James & James, Nursing Reports, 2023). That is a simulator, so nobody got hurt. On the interstate you do not get the version where nobody gets hurt.

Three moves, in order of how much they cost you:

  • Sunglasses on the drive. Free, and they do double duty. Morning sun straight into your eyes tells your clock the day is starting exactly when you need it to end, so the glasses protect the sleep at the other end of the drive as much as they cut the glare.
  • A 20-minute nap before you pull out. If your eyes are heavy in the parking lot, this is the whole answer. Twenty minutes in the recliner costs you twenty minutes. The alternative costs more.
  • Caffeine as an emergency tool, knowingly. It works, and it will take a bite out of your recovery-day sleep. That is a trade worth making to get home safely. It is not one worth making so you can run errands.

Arriving 40 minutes later beats not arriving. And if you are past the point where any of that helps, call somebody.

The recovery-day protocol

Now you are home, and the temptation is enormous and specific: go to bed and get eight hours. Do not. This is the decision that defines whether the 24/48 works for you or grinds you down. Chase eight hours in the morning after a 24 and you take them out of that night.

Sleep pressure is finite, and sleep is what clears it. Spend the whole accumulated load at nine in the morning and by eleven that night there is nothing left to pull you under, so you lie there wide awake. Now you have moved your entire schedule six hours in the wrong direction with a shift day at the far end of it. One bad morning becomes a bad cycle.

What the recovery day actually looks like

The day you come off the truck

A shorter sleep, aligned to your work anchor. The plan engine budgets six hours rather than eight on this day, ending near the same wake time you use on a shift day. Long enough to clear the worst of the debt, short enough to leave you sleepy that night.

One planned nap. Four to five and a half hours after that wake, in the natural dip. Twenty to thirty minutes, or a full ninety. This is what makes the short morning sleep survivable.

Bright light after you wake from it. Within ninety minutes, whatever the sun is doing. This holds your clock in place after a night of pointing it the wrong way.

An early night. Wind-down 60 to 90 minutes before the target. The point of the shorter morning sleep is that this night is available. Take it.

The worked 24/48 schedule runs those numbers against a real pair of anchors, so if you want clock times rather than the rule, that page has them.

One more thing about the recovery day, and it is the part nobody writes down: you will be offered things on this day. Overtime, a callback, a favor, the errand that has waited three days. To everyone who does not work your schedule, the recovery day looks free. Sometimes it looks free to you too. It is not. It is the price of the reset day being any good.

Four

The reset day

The second day off is the only place in the cycle where a full night has room, which makes it the most valuable real estate on your calendar. It is also the day where real life happens.

Both are true, and this guide will not pretend otherwise. This is the day you take the kids somewhere, or go to the gym like a person, or eat dinner with people who work normal hours. That is the point. Sleep is in service of your life and not the other way around, and a plan that turns your only clear day into a rest cure is a plan you abandon inside a month.

So the reset day asks for two things.

Wake on the rest anchor, not whenever. This is where the sleep debt gets paid, so a full opportunity ending on the rest anchor is the assignment. Not noon. Sleep until eleven because you can and the anchor stops meaning anything, and the next tour starts with a body that thinks it is in a different time zone.

Protect the night before you go back. Everything else on this day is negotiable. This is not. Whatever you did with the daylight, the night at the end of the reset day determines what you have to spend on the tour. Caffeine on the cutoff. Wind-down on time. Last real meal three hours before wind-down starts. Nothing exotic — just the ordinary protocol, run properly on the one night of the cycle when running it properly is possible.

Going into the next 24 rested is decided here. Not on the recovery day, where you were doing damage control, and not on the tour, where nothing is decided by you at all.

Five

Anchors on a three-day cycle

Everything on this page is timed from one of two anchors: when you wake, and when you intend to sleep. Never from clock time. The first does most of the work.

Walker's first protocol, in his words: regularity is king. A consistent wake time is the single strongest signal you can send both clocks, and it is the one number the rest of your plan is computed from. (Walker, Guest Series Ep. 2, 6:53) A rotating schedule cannot hold one wake time, so hold one per day-type: a work-day anchor and a rest-day anchor, as close together as your life allows. Land within 30 minutes and count it as a win. Two hours off and the day stops counting as an anchored day.

The 24/48 is unusually kind here. The cycle is three days long, and three days is not enough for your circadian rhythm to move anywhere and stay there — which sounds like bad news and is the opposite. It means you are not trying to shift your clock at all. You are trying to hold it still. Short cycles reward one anchor rather than two, and the closer the rest anchor sits to the work anchor, the less there is to hold.

So: a work anchor and a rest anchor within about an hour of each other. Set the work anchor early enough to reach shift change without a scramble, and the rest anchor no more than an hour later. That is achievable on a 24/48 in a way it is not on a rotating schedule, because you go on duty at the same hour every time.

From the field: put the alarm somewhere high, across the room, so the only way to silence it is to get out of bed — then do not get back in. No trial behind this one; the trick is making the return trip harder than staying up.

Score it fairly, too. Seven sleep opportunities a week, not seven nights in a row. Hit the anchor within thirty minutes and the opportunity counts. Miss one to a 0500 working code and that is one opportunity out of seven, not a streak you have to restart. Five of seven, held for a month, moves more than a perfect week you cannot repeat.

Six

Eating on the 24

Food on a 24 is not really a nutrition question. It is a timing question, and the timing turns out to matter more than anyone expected.

Researchers put people through simulated night shifts and changed one variable: whether they ate during their biological daytime or around the clock like a normal night worker. Same calories, same food, same shifts. The daytime-eating group did not develop the internal circadian misalignment the other group did, and their cardiovascular risk markers stayed flat where the others' climbed (Chellappa, Scheer et al., Nature Communications, 2025). Timing was the whole intervention. Not macros, not clean eating, not a supplement. When you eat.

Now apply that to a station, because a twenty-four-hour tour with no food after dark is not a plan, it is a fantasy. You will be on a call at dinner. You will eat what the crew eats when the crew eats it. So here is the honest version, in order of how realistic it is.

  • Move the real meal earlier in the tour. Eat the substantial one while it is still biological daytime, and keep what comes after dark small. That is the whole finding, applied to a station.
  • If you eat once overnight, make it one low-GI meal. In a crossover trial in night-shift nurses, a single low-glycemic-index meal on shift left glucose profiles close to what they looked like when the nurses ate nothing at all (Soltani et al., J Nutr, 2024). One meal, low GI, early rather than at four in the morning. Not three, not the vending machine.
  • Fasting the overnight is a real option, and not for everyone. Reviews of intermittent fasting in shift workers find it plausible and reasonably tolerated as a way to keep eating inside the biological day, with the honest caveat that the evidence base is thin and the trials are small (review of intermittent fasting in shift workers, 2024). If you are diabetic, pregnant, on medication that needs food, or new to the schedule, this is a conversation with a clinician, not a thing to try because a website mentioned it.

On the recovery day the ordinary rule returns: last real meal at least three hours before wind-down starts. Digestion raises core temperature, and your body has to drop its core temperature to initiate sleep. (Walker on The Drive with Peter Attia, 7:40) The four a.m. plate of leftovers is the classic trap on a 24, and it is a trap because you are bored, not because you are hungry.

Seven

When it is more than the schedule

Some sleep problems are not schedule problems. If you cannot sleep even with the window protected, if you are falling asleep involuntarily on shift, if you snore hard and wake unrefreshed, or if the dread of not sleeping has become its own thing keeping you awake, that is worth a real evaluation. Shift work sleep disorder is a recognized diagnosis, sleep apnea does not care what shift you work, and the best-evidenced treatment for chronic insomnia is not a pill — it is CBT-I, a structured behavioral program.

As of May 2025 a clinician has something they did not have before. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine published its first clinical practice guideline devoted to shift work disorder, which means the advice above stopped being a collection of good studies and became a document a physician can point at (AASM clinical practice guideline, 2025). It suggests bright light during the shift, caffeine before or during it, and a nap before the shift for the sleepiness side; CBT-I for the insomnia side. For daytime sleep specifically, melatonin before it is offered as an option — and the framing matters: that one is conditional and rests on low-certainty evidence, so treat it as something to try under guidance, not a dose to go buy. One recommendation runs the other way. The guideline suggests against benzodiazepines for shift work disorder insomnia. If that is what you were handed, that is a conversation to reopen.

Notice how much of that list you have already read on this page. The pre-shift nap, the light, the caffeine placed early rather than late. The 24/48 protocol is not a workaround for the guideline. It is the guideline, arranged into a three-day cycle.

Is the 24/48 the right schedule at all?

Worth saying, since departments do change this. When researchers followed firefighters through an actual switch from a 24/48 to a 48/96, sleep went up rather than down: departments that made the change gained roughly 23 minutes of sleep per day (longitudinal study of 48/96 schedule change). Over a career that is not nothing.

I would not read it as an argument that everyone should move. It is one comparison, the effect is modest, and a 48 asks something of you that a 24 does not — the second night on duty is a different animal. But if your department is having the conversation, that number is real and it belongs in it.

This guide is education, not medical advice, and the strongest move available sometimes is asking for help.

Common questions

How long should a nap be on a 24-hour shift?

Twenty to thirty minutes when you are on call, because you do not control your own wake. A short nap keeps you in light sleep, so the tones pull you out of something shallow instead of out of deep sleep. If the night is quiet enough to bet on a longer block, a full ninety minutes lets a whole cycle finish. Anything in between is the worst of both.

Why are the two days off on a 24/48 different from each other?

The first day off is a recovery day: you are already awake from shift change and the night is close, so it runs on a shorter sleep opportunity aligned to your shift-day anchor, one planned nap, and an early night. The second day is the reset day, the only place in the cycle where a full night has room. Going into the next 24 rested is decided on that second day, not the first.

Should I sleep as soon as I get home from a 24?

Not for eight hours. Chasing a full night's sleep in the morning after a 24 takes those hours out of the night that follows, and the night is what carries you into the reset day. Take the shorter aligned sleep that ends near your shift-day anchor, add one planned nap in the early afternoon, and go to bed early.

When should I stop drinking coffee on a 24-hour shift?

Eight hours before the sleep opportunity you are protecting. On a 24 that usually puts the last cup somewhere in the evening rather than at 0200 when the wall hits. Caffeine has a half-life around five to six hours, so a cup at three in the morning is still measurably in you when the shift ends.

Is it safe to drive home after a 24-hour shift?

It is the most dangerous part of the tour. In a driving simulator study of nurses on their real post-shift commute, the night-shift group crashed on 11 percent of drives against 7 percent for the day-shift group. If your eyes are heavy, take a 20-minute nap before you leave, wear sunglasses on the drive, or find another way home. Arriving 40 minutes later beats not arriving.

This guide is the rule. Your anchors are the numbers.

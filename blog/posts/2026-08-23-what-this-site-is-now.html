What This Site Is Now

Jaden Schwartz | August 23, 2026 | 5 min read |

For most of its life this site was a machine that wrote about sleep, and I was not really in the room.

It published on a schedule. It covered whatever the topic list handed it that week — gut bacteria, perimenopause, a mocktail that was trending. The posts were competent. Nothing in them was wrong, exactly. And in something like a year of publishing, the whole operation earned about a hundred views and a single subscriber, who I know personally, and who I am fairly sure signed up to be nice.

That is not a traffic problem. That is the site telling me it was not for anyone in particular.

So I read our own numbers, which is a thing I should have done much earlier. Fifty-three posts, and one of them was pulling nearly half of all the traffic the site had ever gotten. One page. It was the shift-work protocol — the piece about working nights, written for people who work nights.

Everything else was the machine talking to no one. The audience had already told us what it wanted, in the only language a website has.

What the site is now

SleepMedic is for people who sleep against the clock. Nights, rotating shifts, twenty-fours, swings, the two-weeks-on schedule that eats a month. Nurses, dispatchers, warehouse and plant floor, security, overnight retail, residents, new parents who did not choose the rotation they are on. If your sleep gets scheduled by somebody else, this is written for you. If it does not, there are a thousand sites that will serve you better, and I mean that without any edge.

Narrowing is the whole decision. The old site tried to be a sleep magazine, and a magazine that covers everyone has to write around the constraint that actually defines your life — that you do not get to pick your bedtime. Almost all sleep advice is quietly written for a person who does. Cut caffeine after two. Keep a consistent bedtime. Wind down when it gets dark. Every one of those assumes a day that starts in the morning and a night nobody else has claimed.

I have worked most of what a schedule can do to a person. Five years in EMS, coming up on it: tens, twelves, days, nights, swings, twenty-fours, thirty-sixes, forty-eights, two on and two off. I have also had the stretch of real insomnia, where I knew all the advice and slept badly anyway, which is its own specific humiliation for someone who works in medicine.

What got me out was not new information. It was deciding sleep was worth defending and then building something I could actually run on a schedule that moves.

The part I got wrong

Here is the honest version, since I would rather say it than have someone notice it later.

The old posts were generated. There was a pipeline, it ran on Mondays and Thursdays, and it wrote about sleep at a volume no person could match. I built it because volume seemed like the thing that worked. Publish enough and something catches.

Nothing caught. Worse, a machine writing on a topic list has no way to know that a piece of advice cannot survive contact with a pager. It will cheerfully tell a medic to keep the first twenty minutes after waking calm and low-stimulus, which is a lovely idea and completely impossible when waking up is the call arriving. That sentence is not a small error. It is the whole reason people stop reading sleep advice.

So the pipeline still exists, but it is fenced. It only works inside shift-work topics, and it fails loudly rather than publishing from memory when it cannot verify a claim. Everything with my name and a first person in it — the essays, the guides, the emails — I write. This one included.

What is actually here

The site stopped being a place that publishes articles and became a place with tools in it. That reordering is most of the pivot.

The plan builder asks six questions about the schedule you really work and returns your anchors, your caffeine cutoff, your light windows, and a nap that fits — computed, not generated, from the same rules I use. Free and complete, no teaser version, no email wall in front of the useful half.

The schedule pages cover nine real rotations, from 24/48 to Dupont to Panama to nights on a three-twelve. Every time on those pages is computed from the rotation, not typed in by hand. The schedule manager puts your actual next fourteen days on real dates, prints, and exports to your calendar.

The tools are small on purpose: a nap calculator, a caffeine cutoff. The field manual is the reasoning underneath all of it, cited to source, including the 2025 shift work disorder guidelines. The guides go deep on one rotation at a time.

All of it is free. There is no paid tier coming, no affiliate links, no gear I get paid to recommend, and no app to download — I have one half-built and I am not going to put a fake button on this site pointing at it. If that changes you will hear it from me directly and not discover it as a paywall.

What I am asking of it

One measure: whether people who work these schedules find this useful enough to hand to the person they work with. That is the whole scoreboard. Not views, which flattered nobody here anyway.

The trade is plain. I write one thing a week that is worth your time, and some of you give me an email address so I can send it. That is the entire business model at the moment, and I would rather it stay that simple for a long while.

There is a version of this site that gets bigger by covering everything. I ran that version for a year. It reached a hundred people and helped, as far as I can tell, none of them.

I would rather be the site that one nurse forwards to another nurse at four in the morning because it finally accounted for the schedule she actually works.

So: this is what it is now. If you sleep against the clock, tell me what your rotation does to you and what nobody has written about yet. I will go find out.
